Cybersecurity for manufacturers — protecting operations, IP, and the factory floor

Manufacturing has become one of the most targeted sectors for ransomware and industrial espionage. The convergence of IT and operational technology — office networks connecting to factory floors — has created vulnerabilities that attackers are actively exploiting.

CyberSense Editorial  ·  6 min read

For decades, manufacturing businesses treated cybersecurity as an office problem — protecting computers, email, and financial systems. The factory floor operated separately, on isolated industrial systems that had little to do with the internet. That separation has largely disappeared.

Today's manufacturing operations run on networked systems — SCADA, industrial control systems, robotics, ERP platforms, and supply chain software that connect the office to the factory and the factory to suppliers and customers. Every connection is a potential attack vector, and attackers have noticed.

Why manufacturing is now the top target

Manufacturing businesses combine several factors that make them attractive targets. They hold valuable intellectual property — product designs, formulations, processes, and tooling that represent years of investment and competitive advantage. They operate on tight margins where production downtime is immediately and severely costly. They have complex supply chains creating many entry points. And they have historically under-invested in cybersecurity relative to their exposure.

The pressure of production schedules means that when ransomware hits a manufacturing facility, the business is under enormous pressure to pay quickly and restore operations — exactly what attackers count on.

A manufacturer that cannot produce for 26 days does not just lose revenue. It misses delivery commitments, triggers contractual penalties, loses customer contracts to competitors, and may permanently damage relationships with key accounts that took years to build.

IT and OT convergence — the new attack surface

The most significant security challenge in manufacturing is the convergence of information technology (IT) — the office network, ERP systems, email, and business applications — with operational technology (OT) — the industrial control systems, programmable logic controllers, SCADA systems, and factory floor equipment that run production.

Historically these were separate. Today they are increasingly connected — for good operational reasons. But that connection means that ransomware entering through an email on the office network can reach production systems, and a compromised industrial device can provide a foothold on the business network.

⚠ OT systems were never designed for security

Industrial control systems, PLCs, and SCADA systems were designed for reliability and safety — not security. Many run software that is decades old, cannot be updated without halting production, and was never designed to be connected to the internet. These systems must be isolated and protected through network architecture, not through patching.

The specific threats manufacturers face

Risk 01

Ransomware targeting production systems

Ransomware that reaches OT systems can halt production entirely — not just locking files but disrupting the industrial control systems that run machinery, manage quality control, and coordinate production processes. Recovery is significantly slower and more complex than in a standard IT environment.

Risk 02

Intellectual property theft

Product designs, manufacturing processes, chemical formulations, and proprietary tooling represent the core competitive advantage of most manufacturers. Nation-state actors and industrial espionage operations specifically target manufacturers to steal this data — often sitting silently on networks for months before extracting it.

Risk 03

Supply chain attacks

Manufacturers sit within complex supply chains — connected to suppliers, logistics providers, and customers through shared systems and data exchanges. A compromised supplier with access to your systems, or a tampered component in your supply chain, can introduce threats directly into your operations.

Risk 04

Industrial espionage and sabotage

Beyond data theft, sophisticated attackers have demonstrated the ability to manipulate industrial processes — altering product quality, causing equipment failures, or disrupting safety systems. While these attacks have historically targeted critical infrastructure, the techniques are increasingly available to a wider range of threat actors.

Protecting operational technology

OT security requires a different approach to IT security. Many of the standard controls — automatic patching, endpoint security software, frequent password changes — are not practical or even possible on industrial systems. The primary protection strategy is isolation and monitoring.

  • Segment OT networks from IT networks — the factory floor should not be able to communicate directly with the office network. A demilitarised zone (DMZ) with strict firewall rules should sit between them.
  • Inventory all OT assets — you cannot protect what you do not know about. A complete inventory of industrial devices, software versions, and network connections is the foundation of OT security.
  • Apply strict remote access controls — remote access to OT systems for maintenance must go through a VPN with MFA, be logged in detail, and be revoked when the maintenance session ends.
  • Monitor OT network traffic — passive monitoring tools can identify unusual communication patterns on industrial networks without disrupting operations.
  • Have tested recovery plans — know in advance exactly how you would restore production systems from backup following a ransomware attack, and test that plan regularly.

Protecting intellectual property

IP theft in manufacturing is often slow and silent. Attackers establish access, map the network, identify where design files and process documentation are stored, and exfiltrate data gradually over weeks or months. By the time it is discovered, the IP is already in the hands of a competitor.

  • Apply strict access controls to design and process files — engineers should access only the files relevant to their current projects
  • Use a dedicated, isolated system for the most sensitive IP — consider air-gapping systems that hold your most valuable proprietary data
  • Monitor for large or unusual file transfers — data exfiltration often involves transferring large volumes of files to external destinations
  • Include IP protection clauses in employment and contractor agreements — and ensure departing employees cannot take IP with them

The NCSC's Active Cyber Defence programme and the Manufacturing Cybersecurity Alliance both provide free guidance and tools specifically for manufacturers. The NCSC's Cyber Essentials scheme provides a practical baseline that addresses the most common vulnerabilities.

Manufacturing cybersecurity checklist

  • IT and OT networks segmented — factory floor isolated from office network
  • Complete inventory of OT assets — all industrial devices and software versions documented
  • Remote access to OT systems via VPN with MFA only — sessions logged and time-limited
  • OT network traffic monitored for unusual patterns
  • Production recovery plan documented and tested — not just written
  • Design and IP files access controlled by project and role
  • Large file transfer monitoring in place
  • Supply chain security assessed — key suppliers evaluated for security practices
  • Staff trained on phishing and social engineering — including shop floor staff
  • Cyber Essentials certification achieved or in progress
  • Offsite backups of business and production system data tested regularly

Manufacturing is the backbone of the economy and increasingly the frontline of cybersecurity. The firms that protect themselves effectively are not necessarily those with the largest IT budgets — they are those that understand the unique intersection of IT and OT risk, take IP protection seriously, and have tested plans for when things go wrong.
