When business owners think about cyberattacks, they imagine it happening to someone else — a large bank, a global retailer, a government agency. The reality is that small businesses are among the most targeted organisations in the world, and the least prepared to survive an attack.

The costs go far beyond whatever a hacker steals. They ripple through every part of your business — and they add up fast.

The hidden cost

Downtime is the silent killer

The single largest cost for most small businesses is not data theft or fines. It is downtime — the days or weeks when your systems are offline, your staff cannot work, and your customers cannot reach you.

The average small business cyberattack causes 21 days of disruption. For a business turning over £500,000 a year, that is roughly £29,000 in lost revenue — before a single pound is spent on recovery.

Every day your systems are down, your competitors are open for business. Customers who cannot reach you do not wait — they find someone else, and many never come back.

A real example

What it looks like in practice

Case study — Small accounting firm, 12 employees

A ransomware attack encrypted every file on the company's server on a Monday morning. Within hours, the entire team was locked out of client records, tax documents, and billing systems.

The firm spent three weeks offline — losing £40,000 in billable work. Incident response cost £18,000. Legal advice and client notification added another £12,000. Two long-term clients moved to competitors during the outage and never returned, representing £35,000 in annual recurring revenue.

Total first-year impact: over £105,000 — from a single attack that exploited one unpatched software vulnerability.

The reputation cost

Trust, once lost, is hard to rebuild

For large corporations, a data breach is a PR crisis. For a small business, it can be an existential one. Customers trust small businesses with their personal information partly because of the personal relationship involved. When that trust is broken, the damage runs deep.

Research consistently shows that a significant share of customers will stop doing business with a company after a breach — and that number is even higher for small, local businesses where trust is a central part of the value proposition.

⚠ The closing statistic

60% of small businesses that suffer a significant cyberattack close their doors within six months — not because the attack destroyed them instantly, but because the accumulated costs of recovery, lost revenue, and damaged reputation made recovery impossible.

The good news

Prevention costs a fraction of recovery

The average small business spends less than £1,000 a year on cybersecurity. The average cost of recovering from an attack is over £150,000. The maths are stark — and they make a compelling case for investing in prevention now, before an attack forces the issue.

The most effective protections are not expensive or complex. They are consistent, well-maintained basics that close the doors attackers rely on.

🔐

Multi-factor authentication

Stops most account takeover attempts at virtually zero cost. One of the highest-impact steps any business can take.

💾

Regular offsite backups

Ransomware loses most of its power when you have clean, recent backups stored separately from your main systems.

🔄

Software updates

Most attacks exploit known vulnerabilities in outdated software. Enabling automatic updates closes these doors automatically.

🎓

Staff security training

A well-trained team is your best defence. Most successful attacks begin with a human mistake that training can prevent.

The question is not whether your business could be targeted. It is whether your business could survive it. The good news is that with the right preparations in place, most attacks can be prevented — and those that do occur can be survived.
