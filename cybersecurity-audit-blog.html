The cybersecurity audit — how to review your own security posture

A cybersecurity audit does not have to mean expensive consultants and lengthy reports. A structured self-assessment, conducted honestly and regularly, reveals the gaps that matter most — and creates the roadmap to close them.

CyberSense Editorial  ·  6 min read

Most businesses know they should review their cybersecurity periodically. Few actually do — and when they do, it is often in response to an incident, a customer requirement, or a regulatory deadline rather than a proactive decision. A self-conducted audit, done well, changes that. It gives you a clear picture of where you stand, what your highest risks are, and what to address first.

This article walks through how to conduct a practical self-audit — structured enough to be rigorous, simple enough to complete without specialist help.

What a self-audit covers

A thorough self-audit reviews eight key areas. None requires specialist tools or deep technical knowledge to assess at the level most small businesses need. What it does require is honesty — the willingness to record a gap accurately rather than assume it is probably fine.

1

Accounts and authentication

Are all accounts protected by MFA? Are there shared accounts? Are former employees' accounts fully disabled? Are admin accounts separated from day-to-day accounts?

2

Devices and endpoints

Is encryption enabled on all laptops? Is endpoint protection installed on all devices? Are operating systems and software up to date? Are personal devices that access business data covered by a policy?

3

Network security

Is the business network protected by a firewall? Is guest Wi-Fi separated from business systems? Are remote access connections secured with MFA and VPN? Are default credentials changed on all network devices?

4

Data and backups

What data does the business hold and where is it stored? Are backups automated, offsite, and tested? Is sensitive data encrypted at rest? Is there a data retention policy?

5

Email and communications

Are SPF, DKIM, and DMARC configured on your domain? Is spam and phishing filtering enabled? Are external email banners in place? Have email accounts been checked for unexpected forwarding rules?

6

Access control

Does each person have access only to what their role requires? Is access reviewed when roles change? Are departing staff fully offboarded across all systems? Are contractor and third-party access permissions current?

7

People and awareness

Have all staff received security awareness training in the past year? Do staff know how to report a suspicious email or incident? Is there a no-blame culture around reporting mistakes?

8

Incident response

Is there a documented plan for what to do if a cyberattack occurs? Do staff know who to contact? Has the plan been tested in the past 12 months? Are ICO and insurance notification obligations understood?

The NCSC Cyber Essentials self-assessment is the single best starting point for any business conducting its first security review. It is free, well-structured, and focuses on the controls that prevent the most common attacks. Completing it gives you a clear gap analysis and, if you pay £300 to certify, an externally recognised credential.

Scoring your findings — prioritising what to fix first

A self-audit will almost always reveal more issues than you can address immediately. Prioritisation is essential. The right order is impact and exploitability — how damaging would this be if exploited, and how easy is it for an attacker to exploit it?

Fix in this order: anything that gives an attacker access to your systems with minimal effort (no MFA, default credentials, unpatched critical vulnerabilities); then anything that could cause catastrophic data loss or business disruption (no tested backups, no encryption on laptops); then configuration and policy gaps that increase risk over time.

⚠ Record gaps honestly — even uncomfortable ones

The value of a self-audit is proportional to its honesty. A gap you record accurately is a gap you can close. A gap you paper over because it is embarrassing or inconvenient is a vulnerability that remains open. Treat the audit as a diagnostic, not a report card. The findings are for your benefit — not for judgement.

How often to audit

For most small businesses, a full self-audit annually and a lighter review of the highest-risk areas quarterly is appropriate. Trigger an unscheduled review after any significant change — a new cloud platform, a major staff change, a security incident, or a change in the regulatory environment. Security posture changes as the business changes, and the audit should track those changes.

Self-audit action checklist

  • All eight audit areas reviewed and findings recorded honestly
  • NCSC Cyber Essentials self-assessment completed
  • Email domain checked on MXToolbox — SPF, DKIM, DMARC verified
  • Business email addresses checked on Have I Been Pwned
  • Website SSL grade checked on SSL Labs
  • Microsoft Secure Score reviewed (if using Microsoft 365)
  • Findings prioritised — highest impact and easiest to exploit fixed first
  • Remediation plan written with owners and deadlines assigned
  • Next audit date scheduled — annual full review, quarterly lighter check
  • Findings reported to business leadership with remediation progress tracked

A self-audit is not a substitute for a professional penetration test or an external security assessment — but it is the foundation that makes those investments worthwhile. A business that knows its own security posture is better prepared, better protected, and better positioned to make the right decisions about where to invest next.
