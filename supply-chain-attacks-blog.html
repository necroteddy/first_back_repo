Supply chain attacks — the threat hiding inside your trusted software

You vetted your software. You trust your vendors. But what if the attacker got in through them — not you? Supply chain attacks are one of the fastest-growing threats facing businesses today.

Most businesses focus their security efforts on their own systems — their network, their devices, their staff. But modern businesses rely on dozens of third-party tools, software packages, and service providers. Every single one is a potential entry point.

A supply chain attack does not target you directly. It targets someone you already trust — and uses that trust to get to you.

How it works

The attack you never see coming

A supply chain attack works by compromising a vendor, software provider, or service partner — then using their trusted access to reach their customers. The attack travels through legitimate channels. It looks like a normal software update or a routine file from a trusted partner.

Because the malicious code arrives from a trusted source, it bypasses many of the defences businesses have in place. Your security tools are not looking for threats coming from software you approved and installed yourself.

Supply chain attacks are particularly dangerous because they exploit trust — the one thing most security systems are not designed to question.

Real world example

When trusted software becomes the weapon

Case study — SolarWinds, 2020

In one of the most significant cyberattacks in history, attackers secretly inserted malicious code into a routine software update from SolarWinds — a widely used IT management platform. The infected update was downloaded by over 18,000 organisations, including government agencies and major corporations.

None of those organisations did anything wrong. They installed what appeared to be a legitimate, digitally signed update from a trusted vendor. The attackers had been inside SolarWinds' own systems for months before anyone noticed.

Total remediation costs across affected organisations ran into billions of dollars. The attack demonstrated that even the most security-conscious organisations are vulnerable when a trusted supplier is compromised.

Why it is growing

Modern businesses are deeply interconnected

The average business today uses over 100 different software tools and services. Each one represents a connection — a supplier who has access to your systems, your data, or your customers. Attackers have noticed this, and they have shifted their strategy accordingly.

Rather than attacking a thousand businesses individually, they attack one supplier and gain access to all of them at once. It is a force multiplier — one breach, thousands of victims.

⚠ The uncomfortable truth

You cannot fully control the security of every vendor you rely on. But you can control how much access each vendor has, how quickly you detect unusual behaviour, and how prepared you are to respond when something goes wrong.

Types of supply chain attacks

It is not just software updates

Supply chain attacks come in several forms — and understanding them helps you know where to focus your defences.

  • Software updates — malicious code inserted into a legitimate update, as in SolarWinds
  • Open source packages — attackers publish malicious packages with names similar to popular libraries
  • Managed service providers — IT companies with access to many clients become high-value targets
  • Hardware tampering — devices pre-loaded with malicious firmware before they reach you
  • Third-party credentials — vendor login details stolen and used to access your systems
What you can do

Questions to ask every vendor

You cannot audit every supplier's internal security. But you can ask the right questions — and the answers will tell you a great deal about whether a vendor takes security seriously.

1

Do you have a formal security programme?

Look for certifications like ISO 27001 or SOC 2. These are independent audits that verify a vendor's security practices meet a recognised standard.

2

How do you handle security in your own software supply chain?

Vendors who rely on open source components or third-party code should be scanning those dependencies regularly for vulnerabilities.

3

What access does your team actually need to our systems?

Apply the principle of least privilege — vendors should only have the minimum access required to do their job, nothing more.

4

How would you notify us in the event of a breach?

A vendor with a clear, rehearsed incident response plan is a vendor that takes security seriously. Vague answers are a red flag.

5

Have you experienced a security incident in the past two years?

Past incidents are not necessarily disqualifying — how a vendor responded to them tells you far more than whether they happened.

Supply chain security checklist
  • ✓ Audit which third-party tools and vendors have access to your systems
  • ✓ Apply least privilege — vendors get only the access they genuinely need
  • ✓ Ask vendors about their security certifications and practices
  • ✓ Monitor for unusual activity from third-party connections
  • ✓ Keep software updated — but verify updates come from official sources
  • ✓ Have a response plan for when a trusted vendor is compromised
  • ✓ Review vendor access regularly and remove access that is no longer needed

Supply chain attacks are difficult to prevent entirely — because they exploit the trust that makes modern business possible. But businesses that ask the right questions, limit vendor access, and monitor for unusual behaviour are far better positioned to detect and contain them before serious damage is done.
