Password safety and management — the foundation of account security

Despite years of advances in authentication technology, passwords remain the most common way businesses protect their accounts — and the most common way those accounts are compromised. Getting password management right is one of the simplest, cheapest, and most impactful security improvements available.

CyberSense Editorial  ·  6 min read

Password-related issues remain at the centre of the vast majority of account compromises — not because passwords are inherently weak, but because the way people manage them is. Reused passwords, weak passwords, passwords shared over insecure channels, and passwords that exist nowhere except a person's memory all create risk that is entirely preventable with the right tools and habits.

The good news is that fixing password security does not require sophisticated technology or significant investment. A password manager and a clear set of policies addresses the vast majority of password-related risk for any business.

Why password reuse is the single biggest risk

Password reuse is the practice that turns a single breach into many. When a service you use is breached and your password is stolen, attackers do not stop at that one service. They test the same email and password combination against banking sites, email providers, social media, and business systems — a technique called credential stuffing. If you reused that password anywhere else, those accounts are now compromised too.

This is why a breach you have never heard of — a forum you signed up to years ago, a service that has since shut down — can still lead to your business email account being compromised today. The connection is not a sophisticated attack. It is simply password reuse, exploited automatically and at scale.

Check haveibeenpwned.com for any email address used across your business. If any has appeared in a breach, and the password from that breach was reused anywhere else, every one of those accounts should be treated as potentially compromised and have its password changed immediately.

What makes a password actually strong

Traditional password advice — eight characters, a capital letter, a number, a symbol — produces passwords that are both hard for humans to remember and relatively easy for modern cracking tools to guess, because people respond to these rules predictably (Password1!, Summer2024!).

Current best practice, reflected in NCSC and NIST guidance, favours length and randomness over complexity rules. A long passphrase of random, unrelated words is both easier to remember and dramatically harder to crack than a short complex string.

  • Length matters more than complexity — a 16+ character passphrase is far stronger than an 8-character complex password
  • Random word combinations work well — "correct horse battery staple" style passphrases (but generate your own, do not use that exact example) are memorable and strong
  • Uniqueness matters more than memorability — every account needs a different password; this is only practical with a password manager
  • Generated random passwords are strongest — for accounts you do not need to type manually, a fully random password generated and stored by your password manager is the strongest option
⚠ Forced regular password changes are now considered bad practice

NCSC guidance has moved away from forcing regular password changes (e.g. every 90 days) for accounts protected by MFA, because this practice tends to result in weaker, more predictable passwords (Password1, Password2, Password3) as people search for something easy to remember and type. A strong, unique password protected by MFA, changed only when there is reason to believe it has been compromised, is now the recommended approach.

Password managers — solving the problem properly

A password manager is the single most effective tool for solving password security at scale. It generates strong, unique, random passwords for every account, stores them encrypted, and autofills them when needed — removing the human tendency toward weak, memorable, reused passwords entirely.

Deploying a password manager across your business

For a business, deploying a password manager is not just about individual convenience — it provides centralised policy enforcement, secure password sharing for shared accounts, visibility into weak or reused passwords across the team, and immediate revocation of access when someone leaves.

  • Choose a business tier with admin controls — this allows you to enforce policy, see (not the passwords themselves, but security health metrics like reuse and weakness), and manage access centrally
  • Use secure sharing for shared accounts — never send a password by email or chat; password managers allow secure sharing without ever exposing the plaintext password to the recipient if needed
  • Enable the security health/breach monitoring features — most business password managers will flag weak, reused, or breached passwords across your organisation
  • Make adoption mandatory, not optional — a password manager only protects the accounts it is used for; partial adoption leaves gaps

Passkeys — the emerging alternative

Passkeys represent the most significant shift in authentication in decades — replacing passwords entirely with cryptographic key pairs tied to your device and verified by biometrics or a PIN. They cannot be phished, cannot be reused across sites, and cannot be stolen in a data breach in any form that is useful to an attacker.

Major platforms — Microsoft, Google, Apple — now support passkeys, and adoption is accelerating. For businesses, passkeys are not yet a complete replacement for password managers across every service, but they represent the direction authentication is heading, and enabling them where available — particularly for your most critical accounts — provides meaningfully stronger protection than even a strong password with MFA.

Where available, enable passkeys for your most critical accounts — Microsoft 365, Google Workspace administrator accounts, and your password manager itself. Passkeys eliminate the password entirely for that login, removing an entire category of risk.

Password security checklist

  • Business password manager deployed with mandatory adoption across all staff
  • Every account has a unique, randomly generated or long passphrase password
  • Shared accounts use secure sharing features — never sent via email or chat
  • Security health / breach monitoring enabled in the password manager
  • Business email domains checked on Have I Been Pwned
  • MFA enabled on all accounts as the primary defence against credential compromise
  • Forced regular password changes removed for MFA-protected accounts
  • Passkeys enabled where available for the most critical accounts
  • Password manager access revoked promptly as part of offboarding
  • Staff trained on why password reuse is dangerous — not just told not to do it

Passwords are not going away soon, despite the rise of passkeys and biometrics. Until they do, the businesses best protected are those that have removed human memory and habit from the equation entirely — generating, storing, and using strong unique passwords through a password manager, backed by MFA on every account. It is one of the cheapest, simplest, and most effective security investments available.
