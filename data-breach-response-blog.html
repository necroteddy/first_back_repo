CyberSense Editorial April 2026 6 min read

Discovering a data breach is one of the most stressful moments a business owner can face. Your instinct may be to panic, to cover it up, or to wait and see if it really is as bad as it looks. All three reactions will make things significantly worse.

What you need instead is a clear, calm plan. Here is exactly what to do — step by step — from the moment you discover a breach to the weeks that follow.

Phase one — First hour

Contain the breach immediately

Your first priority is to stop more data from leaving. Do not shut everything down — that can destroy evidence you will need later. Instead, isolate the affected systems by disconnecting them from the network while keeping them powered on.

Change passwords on any accounts that may have been compromised. Revoke access tokens, disable affected user accounts, and contact your IT team or security provider right away.

⚠ Critical — Do this first

Do not wipe or reset affected systems yet. Doing so destroys the forensic evidence needed to understand what happened, who is responsible, and what data was taken.

Phase two — First 24 hours

Understand what actually happened

Once the breach is contained, you need to investigate. Work with your IT team or a cybersecurity specialist to understand how the attacker got in, what systems they accessed, and what data they took or exposed.

Document everything you find — timestamps, affected files, access logs, and the sequence of events. This documentation is essential for legal compliance, insurance claims, and preventing a repeat.

If you do not have in-house cybersecurity expertise, bring in a specialist. Many cyber insurance policies cover the cost of an incident response team — check your policy immediately.

Phase three — Days 1 to 3

Notify the people who need to know

This is the step most business owners dread — but it is also one of the most legally important. Most data protection laws require you to notify regulators within a strict timeframe. Under GDPR, that window is 72 hours. Under many US state laws, it is similar.

You also need to notify affected customers — clearly, honestly, and promptly. Tell them what happened, what data was involved, what you are doing about it, and what they should do to protect themselves.

⚠ Legal risk

Failing to notify regulators within the required timeframe is itself a violation — and can result in significantly larger fines than the breach itself. Do not delay notification while waiting for the full picture to emerge.

What not to do

The four mistakes that make breaches far worse

In the chaos of a breach, businesses often make decisions they later regret. These four mistakes appear again and again — and each one dramatically increases the damage.

✕

Staying silent

Hoping nobody notices is not a strategy. Regulators and customers will find out — the question is whether they hear it from you first.

✕

Wiping systems immediately

Destroying evidence makes it impossible to understand what happened — and looks like an attempt to conceal the breach.

✕

Paying ransom without advice

Paying does not guarantee data recovery, may be illegal in some jurisdictions, and signals that your business will pay again.

✕

Going it alone

Breach response requires legal, technical, and communications expertise. Trying to handle all three without specialists rarely ends well.

Phase four — Ongoing recovery

Rebuild — stronger than before

Once the immediate crisis is managed, the real work begins. Fix the vulnerabilities that allowed the breach. Audit all your systems. Update your security policies. Retrain your team.

Then communicate openly with your customers about what you have changed. Businesses that respond to breaches transparently and decisively often emerge with their reputation intact — sometimes even stronger than before.

A breach is not just a crisis — it is also a forcing function. It reveals exactly where your weaknesses are and gives you the clearest possible motivation to fix them.

Breach response checklist
  • ✓ Isolate affected systems without wiping them
  • ✓ Change all compromised passwords and access tokens
  • ✓ Contact your IT team or cybersecurity specialist
  • ✓ Document everything — logs, timelines, affected data
  • ✓ Check your cyber insurance policy for coverage
  • ✓ Notify regulators within the required legal timeframe
  • ✓ Notify affected customers honestly and promptly
  • ✓ Fix vulnerabilities and update your security policies

No business plans to have a breach. But every business should plan for one. The companies that recover fastest are not the ones that had perfect security — they are the ones that knew exactly what to do when things went wrong.
