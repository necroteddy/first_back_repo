Dark web monitoring — finding out if your data has already been stolen

By the time a data breach makes the news, the stolen data has usually been circulating on dark web markets for weeks or months. Dark web monitoring gives you early warning — before criminals have had time to use your credentials, your customer data, or your employees' details against you.

CyberSense Editorial  ·  5 min read

The average time between a data breach occurring and the affected organisation discovering it is 194 days. In that window, stolen credentials are sold, customer data is listed on underground markets, and the information is used to launch further attacks. Dark web monitoring shortens that window — alerting you when your data appears in places it should never be, before the damage compounds.

For most small businesses, dark web monitoring is not a complex or expensive undertaking. Several free tools cover the most important use cases, and paid services extend coverage significantly for a modest monthly cost.

What the dark web actually is

The dark web is a part of the internet that is not indexed by standard search engines and requires specific software — typically the Tor browser — to access. It hosts both legitimate privacy-focused services and a significant criminal economy including stolen data markets, credential shops, ransomware-as-a-service platforms, and forums where cybercriminals buy, sell, and exchange tools and information.

When a company suffers a data breach — whether through a direct attack or through a breach at a supplier — the stolen data frequently ends up on these markets. Credentials are sold by the millions. Customer databases are auctioned. Internal documents appear on ransomware leak sites when organisations refuse to pay.

You do not need to access the dark web yourself to monitor it. Reputable monitoring services index dark web markets, forums, and leak sites and alert you when your data appears — without you needing to visit any of those environments directly.

What dark web monitoring looks for

Different monitoring services cover different types of exposure. Understanding what you most need to monitor helps you choose the right tools.

  • Email addresses and credentials — the most common exposure. Your employees' email addresses and passwords from breached services, ready for use in credential stuffing attacks against your business systems.
  • Domain mentions — your company domain appearing in criminal forums, leak sites, or data sale listings — an early indicator that your organisation has been targeted or breached.
  • Customer data — customer email addresses, phone numbers, or payment details appearing in leaked databases, indicating a breach of your own systems or a supplier.
  • Executive personal data — home addresses, personal email addresses, and phone numbers of senior staff — used in targeted attacks, extortion, and highly personalised phishing.
  • Source code and documents — internal documents, source code, or credentials appearing on paste sites or leak forums, indicating an insider threat or external breach.

What to do when you get an alert

Receiving a dark web alert does not automatically mean you have been breached. Many alerts relate to credentials stolen from third-party services — LinkedIn, Adobe, or a cloud tool — rather than from your own systems. But every alert warrants a response.

  • Identify the source — which breach did the data come from? HIBP and most monitoring tools will tell you which known breach the data appeared in.
  • Reset the affected credentials immediately — even if the password was from a third-party service, if any of your employees reused it on business systems, those systems are now at risk. Change the password, check for unusual activity, and verify MFA is in place.
  • Check for reuse across systems — credential stuffing works because people reuse passwords. An alert about one account should trigger a review of password hygiene across the business.
  • Investigate if the data relates to your own systems — if customer data or internal documents appear that could only have come from your systems, treat it as a confirmed breach and begin your incident response process.
  • Notify affected individuals if required — if personal data has been exposed, GDPR notification obligations may apply.

Prevention — the best response to a dark web alert

Dark web monitoring is detective, not preventive — it tells you data has already been exposed, not that it is about to be. The controls that prevent credentials from being useful when stolen are MFA (which renders stolen passwords useless without the second factor) and a password manager (which ensures unique passwords so a breach of one service does not compromise others). Monitoring and prevention work together — monitoring tells you when something has gone wrong; MFA and password hygiene limit the damage it can do.

Dark web monitoring checklist

  • Have I Been Pwned checked for your business domain — all breaches reviewed
  • HIBP domain monitoring set up — automatic alerts for future breaches
  • All staff using a password manager — unique passwords across all services
  • MFA enabled on all accounts — stolen passwords useless without second factor
  • Alert response process defined — who acts on alerts and what they do
  • Paid dark web monitoring in place if handling sensitive customer data at scale
  • Customer data breach response process documented — GDPR notification procedure clear
  • Executive personal data monitored — senior staff names and domains included

Your data may already be on the dark web. The question is whether you know about it and have acted — or whether you will find out when a criminal uses it. Dark web monitoring does not prevent breaches. It ensures that when your data appears somewhere it should not, you find out first and respond before the window for effective action closes.
