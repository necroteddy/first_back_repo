What it is

Ransomware explained plainly

Ransomware is malicious software that encrypts your files — making them completely unreadable without a decryption key. Once your files are encrypted, a message appears demanding payment — usually in cryptocurrency — in exchange for the key to unlock them.

The attack is designed to leave you with no choice. Every file your business depends on — customer records, financial data, contracts, emails — is suddenly inaccessible. The clock is ticking, the pressure is enormous, and the criminals are counting on panic to make you pay.

Ransomware attackers do not just lock your files. Many now steal copies of your data first — threatening to publish it publicly if you do not pay. This is called double extortion.

How it gets in

The three most common entry points

Ransomware does not appear from nowhere. It enters through specific, well-understood vulnerabilities — and closing these doors is the most effective way to protect your business.

  • Malicious email attachments — a staff member opens an infected file and the malware silently installs itself
  • Compromised credentials — stolen or guessed passwords give attackers direct access to your systems via remote desktop or VPN
  • Unpatched software — known vulnerabilities in outdated systems give attackers a ready-made way in
⚠ The waiting game

Attackers often sit inside a network for weeks before triggering the ransomware. They use this time to map your systems, find your backups, and maximise the damage when they finally strike.

Should you pay?

The ransom dilemma — and why paying is rarely the answer

When ransomware hits, the immediate instinct for many business owners is to pay and make it go away. The reality is far more complicated.

Paying the ransom does not guarantee you will get your files back. Nearly half of businesses that pay never fully recover their data. Paying also marks your business as one that will pay — making you a target for repeat attacks. And in some jurisdictions, paying ransoms to certain criminal groups may be illegal.

⚠ Before you decide

If you are hit by ransomware, do not pay immediately. Contact a cybersecurity incident response specialist first. They may be able to recover your data without payment — and can advise on your legal obligations before any money changes hands.

The real defence

Why backups are your most powerful weapon

Ransomware loses most of its power when you have clean, recent backups stored somewhere the attacker cannot reach. If your data is safely backed up and you can restore from a point before the infection, you can recover without paying a penny.

The critical word is somewhere the attacker cannot reach. Backups stored on the same network as your main systems will be encrypted along with everything else. Your backups need to be offline, offsite, or in a cloud environment that is completely separate from your main infrastructure.

The 3-2-1 backup rule: keep 3 copies of your data, on 2 different types of storage, with 1 stored completely offsite. A backup that ransomware can reach is not a backup.

Prevention plan

How to protect your business from ransomware

Ransomware is serious — but it is also highly preventable. Businesses that follow these steps are dramatically less likely to be hit, and far more likely to recover quickly if they are.

1

Implement the 3-2-1 backup rule

Three copies, two storage types, one offsite. Test your backups regularly — a backup you have never restored from is an untested assumption.

2

Keep all software patched and updated

Enable automatic updates across all devices and software. Unpatched systems are the single most common ransomware entry point after email.

3

Enable multi-factor authentication everywhere

Stolen credentials are a primary ransomware delivery method. MFA stops attackers from using stolen passwords to access your systems remotely.

4

Train your team to spot malicious emails

Most ransomware still arrives via email. Regular, short training sessions dramatically reduce the chance of an employee triggering an infection.

5

Segment your network

If ransomware gets in, network segmentation limits how far it can spread. Keep critical systems isolated from general business systems.

6

Have a written ransomware response plan

Know in advance exactly what you will do if ransomware hits — who to call, what to isolate, when to notify authorities. Decisions made under panic are rarely good ones.

Ransomware protection checklist
  • ✓ 3-2-1 backups in place — with one copy completely offsite
  • ✓ Backups tested and verified to restore correctly
  • ✓ All software and systems kept up to date automatically
  • ✓ Multi-factor authentication on all accounts and remote access
  • ✓ Staff trained to recognise suspicious emails and attachments
  • ✓ Network segmentation in place for critical systems
  • ✓ Written ransomware response plan ready before it is needed
  • ✓ Cyber insurance policy reviewed for ransomware coverage

Ransomware is one of the most serious threats your business faces — but it is not inevitable. Businesses that back up consistently, patch diligently, and train their teams are far better positioned to prevent an attack and recover from one if it happens. The time to prepare is before the ransom note appears.
