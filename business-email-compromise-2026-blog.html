Business email compromise — the fraud hiding in your inbox

No malware. No ransomware. No technical exploit. Business email compromise is pure social engineering through a trusted channel — and it costs businesses more money globally than any other category of cybercrime.

CyberSense Editorial  ·  6 min read

Business email compromise (BEC) is deceptively simple in concept and devastatingly effective in practice. An attacker either compromises a legitimate email account or creates a convincing lookalike, then uses that trusted channel to manipulate someone into transferring money, changing payment details, or disclosing sensitive information. There is often no malicious code involved at all — just patient observation and a carefully timed, convincingly worded email.

The FBI's Internet Crime Complaint Centre consistently ranks BEC as the costliest category of cybercrime by financial loss — ahead of ransomware, ahead of data breaches, ahead of every technically sophisticated attack type. The reason is straightforward: it works, it scales, and it exploits trust rather than vulnerabilities in code.

The four common BEC scenarios

Scenario 01

CEO fraud

An email appearing to come from the CEO or a senior executive instructs a member of the finance team to make an urgent, confidential payment — often citing a sensitive acquisition, a regulatory matter, or a deal that cannot be discussed openly. The combination of authority and confidentiality discourages the recipient from verifying through normal channels.

Scenario 02

Vendor/supplier impersonation

An email appearing to come from a known supplier advises that their bank account details have changed, requesting that future payments — including one already due — be sent to the new account. The relationship and history of legitimate communication makes this request appear routine.

Scenario 03

Attorney/professional impersonation

An email impersonating a lawyer, accountant, or other professional adviser involved in a confidential or time-sensitive matter — instructing urgent action, typically a payment, and leveraging the authority and confidentiality typical of professional communications to discourage verification.

Scenario 04

Payroll diversion

An email appearing to come from an employee requests that their salary payment details be updated before the next pay run. The request appears entirely routine — HR teams process genuine versions of this request constantly — making it easy to action without verification.

How attackers gain the trusted channel

BEC attacks succeed because the email appears to come from a trusted source. There are three primary ways attackers achieve this convincing appearance.

Direct account compromise. The attacker gains access to a real email account — through phishing, credential stuffing, or password reuse — and sends fraudulent messages from the genuine account. This is the most convincing form of BEC because the email is, in every technical respect, genuine. MFA is the primary defence against this scenario.

Domain spoofing. The attacker sends email that appears to come from a legitimate domain by manipulating the "From" header, exploiting the absence of email authentication. DMARC, SPF, and DKIM are the primary defences against this scenario.

Lookalike domains. The attacker registers a domain that closely resembles the legitimate one — substituting a letter, adding a hyphen, using a different top-level domain — and sends email from it. A recipient who does not examine the sender address carefully may not notice the difference. Careful inspection of sender addresses is the primary defence.

Register common lookalike variations of your own domain — with a hyphen, common misspellings, alternative TLDs — to prevent attackers from registering them for use against your customers and suppliers. This is a low-cost, proactive defence many businesses overlook.

Why BEC succeeds even against careful people

BEC attacks are effective specifically because they exploit the psychology of trusted, routine business communication rather than relying on a technical flaw. The request often genuinely resembles something that would normally happen — a bank detail change, an urgent payment, a confidential instruction. The attacker has frequently done reconnaissance, studying the target's real communication style, organisational structure, and ongoing transactions, sometimes from previously compromised email threads.

The defining characteristic of successful BEC is that it never asks the victim to do something they have not done before — it asks them to do something routine, just this once, slightly differently than usual.

⚠ The single most important rule: verify by phone, every time

Any request to change payment or bank account details, or any unusual or urgent payment request, must be verified by telephone — using a phone number you already have on file, never a number provided in the email itself. This rule applies regardless of the seniority of the apparent sender, the urgency of the request, or how routine it appears. It is the single control that defeats the vast majority of BEC attempts, and it costs nothing beyond a two-minute phone call.

Technical defences against BEC

  • MFA on every email account — prevents the direct account compromise scenario, the most convincing form of BEC
  • DMARC, DKIM, and SPF configured and enforced — prevents domain spoofing; ensure DMARC policy is set to quarantine or reject, not just monitor
  • External email banners — flag emails originating from outside your organisation, helping staff spot impersonation attempts at a glance
  • Inbox rule monitoring — compromised accounts often have hidden forwarding rules; review regularly for unexpected rules forwarding mail externally
  • Lookalike domain registration and monitoring — register obvious variations of your domain; monitor for newly registered lookalikes using free tools like dnstwist

Procedural defences — the human layer

  • Mandatory phone verification for payment and bank detail changes — written policy, no exceptions, applied to every level of seniority
  • Dual authorisation for payments above a threshold — a second person must independently approve any significant transfer
  • Defined escalation for urgent or confidential requests — a clear process for handling requests that claim confidentiality or urgency as a reason to bypass normal verification
  • Staff training specific to BEC scenarios — generic phishing training does not always cover the social engineering specifics of CEO fraud, vendor impersonation, and payroll diversion

If you suspect a BEC attempt or have already paid

If you receive a suspicious payment request, do not reply to the email — verify by phone using a known number, and report the email to your IT team. If a fraudulent payment has already been made, act within minutes, not hours: contact your bank immediately to request a recall of the funds (banks can sometimes intercept transfers within the first hours), report to Action Fraud, and notify your cyber insurer. Speed dramatically improves the chance of fund recovery.

Business email compromise checklist

  • MFA enabled on all email accounts
  • DMARC, SPF, and DKIM configured with enforcing policy (not monitor-only)
  • External email banner enabled
  • Written policy: all bank detail changes verified by phone using on-file number
  • Dual authorisation required for payments above a defined threshold
  • Staff trained specifically on CEO fraud, vendor impersonation, and payroll diversion scenarios
  • Inbox forwarding rules audited regularly across all accounts
  • Common lookalike domains of your business registered or monitored
  • Bank's fraud recall process known in advance — contact details saved
  • Incident response plan includes immediate steps for suspected BEC

Business email compromise does not need to defeat your firewall, exploit a vulnerability, or deploy malware. It only needs to convince one person, for one moment, to act on a request that looks routine. The defence is not more technology — it is a habit of verification that becomes automatic, applied consistently regardless of pressure, seniority, or urgency.
